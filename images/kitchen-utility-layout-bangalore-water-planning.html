Water Planning

Why Your Modular Layout Should Plan for Bangalore's Water Reality, Not Just the Brochure Version

Tanker dependency, RO wastage, and intermittent BWSSB supply mean your kitchen and utility area need plumbing provisions that generic modular kitchen guides never mention.

Wood & Co. Interiors · September 2026 · 7 min read

Search for "modular kitchen design" and every guide assumes the same thing: water arrives through the wall, on demand, indefinitely. That's a reasonable assumption in most Indian cities. It is not a safe assumption in Bangalore, where tanker trucks are a routine part of household logistics, resident WhatsApp groups track BWSSB supply days like a weather forecast, and groundwater has been drawn down for over a decade. None of that shows up in a standard modular kitchen layout, and it should.

This isn't a plumbing guide, and it isn't a lecture about conserving water. It's a practical case for a few specific layout and access decisions, made at the design stage, that cost close to nothing today and save you from cutting open finished cabinetry two years from now. Think of it as due diligence you'd want from any designer who actually understands the city they're building in, rather than one working off a template written somewhere else.

Why generic modular kitchen guides miss this entirely

Most modular kitchen content is written for, or copied from, markets where municipal water pressure is a given. The layouts optimise for storage, workflow, and finish, which are the right priorities everywhere, but they treat the water supply itself as invisible infrastructure that just works. In Bangalore, it isn't. A large share of apartments, especially outside the core BWSSB network, depend partly or fully on tanker refills, and even connected buildings see supply that varies by day and by season.

A Detail Most Kitchens Get Wrong

RO purifiers, now standard in most Bangalore kitchens given groundwater quality concerns, typically waste two to three litres for every litre they purify. That waste line has to go somewhere, and in most modular kitchens it's routed straight to the drain behind a sealed cabinet, with no thought given to whether that water could be caught and reused for mopping or plants instead.

Why this matters even if your building feels fine right now

Bangalore's water supply comes from two main sources: Cauvery river allocation, which is fixed and doesn't grow with the city, and groundwater, which has been drawn down for decades faster than it recharges. Newer layouts on the city's outskirts, where a lot of ongoing construction is happening, often sit outside the core BWSSB pipeline network entirely and depend on borewells or tanker supply as a matter of routine, not emergency. Even well-connected buildings see their BWSSB allocation fluctuate with the season, since supply is tightest exactly when demand peaks, in the hotter, drier months before the monsoon.

None of this is a reason to panic about a kitchen renovation. It's a reason to treat water access as infrastructure you design around, the same way you'd design around an electrical panel or a structural column, rather than as an afterthought you deal with only when a tanker fails to show up on the day you needed it.

Five things your utility area layout should plan for

None of these require an expensive water-treatment installation on day one. They require the design to leave room and access for one later, instead of assuming it will never be needed.

  • RO purifier waste-water routing. Instead of a waste line disappearing into a sealed cabinet, position the purifier so its outlet is reachable, with space for a small catchment container underneath. Even manually reusing that water for mopping recovers a meaningful share of what would otherwise go straight down the drain.
  • Sump and tank refill accessibility. If your building relies even partly on tanker refills, the inlet point needs a clear, unobstructed path from the road or driveway. Don't let a wardrobe, false ceiling drop, or storage unit box in a refill point that a tanker operator needs to reach quickly.
  • Washing machine plumbing. A direct-line connection assumes constant pressure. Where supply is inconsistent, a small buffer arrangement, feeding from a tank rather than directly off the mains, keeps the machine usable on low-pressure days. This is a plumbing decision, but the cabinetry around it needs to leave room for it.
  • Greywater reuse provision. Even if you don't install a reuse system immediately, a blank plumbing knockout from the washing machine or kitchen sink outlet costs very little to leave in place during construction. Adding one later, after the floor and cabinetry are finished, costs considerably more.
  • Equipment access panels. Water softeners, purifiers, and pressure pumps all need periodic servicing. Building them into a sealed unit that has to be partially dismantled for a technician to reach a filter is a design mistake that shows up the first time something needs replacing, not at handover.

Standard layout vs a Bangalore-adapted layout

The difference is rarely about spending more. It's about where a wall gets left blank, where a panel opens instead of staying sealed, and where a unit sits six inches to one side of where it would otherwise go.

What to tell your interior designer or plumber before finalising the layout

You don't need to become a plumbing expert to get this right. You need to ask a few pointed questions before the layout is frozen, while changes are still free:

  • "Where does the RO waste line go, and can I reach it?" If the honest answer is "behind that sealed panel," ask for it to be repositioned.
  • "Is our sump refill point going to stay accessible once the cabinetry goes in?" Walk the layout with this specific question in mind, since it's easy to miss on a 2D plan.
  • "Can we leave a blank plumbing knockout near the washing machine outlet, even if we're not using it yet?" This is the cheapest insurance in the entire list.
  • "Does the water treatment equipment have a proper access panel, or is it sealed in?" A five-minute filter change shouldn't require dismantling a unit.
  • "If our building ever needs a sump-level buffer tank added, is there space reserved for it?" Even a modest, unused cabinet footprint reserved today is far easier than finding space in an already-finished utility area two years in.

None of these questions require your designer to be a plumbing specialist either. They just need to be asked, and answered, before the layout goes to manufacturing rather than after the cabinetry is installed.

Frequently asked questions

Does this mean I need a water softener or RO reuse system installed right away?

No. The point isn't to force every water fixture into your first fit-out, it's to leave the physical space and plumbing access so you can add one later without breaking open finished cabinetry. A blank knockout costs almost nothing at design stage; retrofitting one later means cutting into an installed unit.

My building has a dedicated tanker refill point on the terrace. Does any of this still apply?

Less, but the RO wastage and access-panel points still matter inside the flat itself regardless of where your water ultimately comes from. Tanker dependency mainly changes how much you prioritise sump-level and pressure buffering in the utility area design.

Isn't RO waste water a plumbing problem, not an interior design one?

The plumbing itself is the plumber's job, but where that waste line physically goes, and whether the cabinetry underneath it is designed to be opened without dismantling the unit, is an interior design decision made at the layout stage, well before the plumber is even on site.

Will any of this add significantly to my quotation?

Usually not. Most of these are layout and access decisions rather than added materials, for example leaving a panel instead of a sealed shutter, or positioning a unit six inches to the left of a wall outlet. The cost difference is close to zero when planned at the design stage; it's only expensive as an afterthought.

The bottom line

None of this is about solving Bangalore's water situation from a kitchen cabinet. It's about not designing your home as if the situation doesn't exist. A modular layout that ignores tanker dependency, RO wastage, and access for future equipment isn't wrong on the day it's installed, it's wrong two or three years later, when you need to change something and discover the design never left room for it. The fix costs nothing at the design stage and a great deal after the cabinetry is sealed shut.
