How Many Site Visits Does Your Modular Project Actually Need, and When

Not enough visits and nobody catches problems early. Too many and you're paying for hand-holding. Here's the real schedule, stage by stage.

Wood & Co. Interiors · October 2026 · 7 min read

Two kinds of complaints show up about modular interior projects, and they're exact opposites. One homeowner says their designer visited once at the start, vanished for six weeks, and reappeared only for the final reveal. Another says their designer kept showing up unannounced, which they found less reassuring and more like a sign nothing was actually under control. Both are describing the same underlying problem: nobody told them what a normal visit schedule should look like, so they had no way to judge whether what they were getting was too little, too much, or exactly right.

There's a real answer to this, and it doesn't depend on how attentive your designer feels like being that week. A modular kitchen or wardrobe project has a small number of points where a physical site visit is genuinely necessary, and a larger number of points where it genuinely isn't. Knowing which is which is useful whether you're hiring us or anyone else.

Why this varies so much from one studio to another

Part of the confusion comes from studios that advertise "regular site visits" without ever defining what regular means, which lets it mean almost anything depending on how busy that month is. The other part comes from homeowners reasonably assuming that more visits equals more care, when in practice a competent installation team needs defined checkpoints, not a supervisor standing over them for six weeks.

A Useful Reframe

The right question isn't "how many times will you visit." It's "which specific visits are non-negotiable, and what happens at each one." A studio that can answer that immediately has actually thought about their process. A studio that answers with a vague number hasn't.

The real visit schedule, stage by stage

  • Site measurement visit (non-negotiable). This is where the design is actually built around your real walls, not a builder's floor plan. Electrical points, plumbing position, beam drops, and window sills all get recorded here. Everything downstream depends on this being accurate.
  • Design presentation (can be remote). Reviewing layouts, 3D renders, and material samples doesn't require anyone on-site. A video call or an in-studio meeting works just as well, and for NRI clients this is almost always done remotely anyway.
  • Pre-manufacturing site-readiness check (non-negotiable). Before your order goes into production, someone should confirm the site still matches what was measured. Civil work sometimes shifts between the measurement visit and the order date, plastering finished differently, an electrical point moved, and manufacturing to outdated measurements is one of the most common causes of an installation that doesn't quite fit.
  • Mid-installation checkpoint (recommended, not daily). Once installation begins, a checkpoint partway through lets you catch alignment or fit issues while they're still easy to correct, rather than discovering them at handover when everything is already fixed in place.
  • Final handover and snag-list visit (non-negotiable). This is where hinges, drawer action, shutter alignment, and finish quality get checked item by item before you sign off, not glanced at in passing.

What this looks like in practice

For a straightforward single kitchen or wardrobe, that's realistically three to four visits with real purpose, not a dozen, and not two. The gaps between them should still be filled with something, a photo update, a short video, a message confirming a milestone was hit, even when nobody's physically on-site that week. Silence between visits is what makes people assume nothing's happening, even when the project is actually on track.

Why this looks different in Bangalore versus Mangalore

The five checkpoints above stay the same in both cities, but the logistics around them don't. In Bangalore, traffic alone can turn a same-day visit into something that needs to be scheduled a week out, so studios juggling multiple projects across the city sometimes let gaps stretch longer than they should between checkpoints simply because getting across town is its own small project. At our Kulur studio, the geography is more forgiving, but the trade-off is a smaller pool of specialised installation crews, so a checkpoint might get pushed by a day or two while the right team becomes available rather than because anyone's being careless.

Neither of these is a reason to accept a vague schedule. It's a reason to ask, specifically, how your designer plans to work around their own city's logistics so that none of the three non-negotiable visits actually slip.

What actually goes wrong when a checkpoint gets skipped

The failure mode isn't dramatic, which is part of why it's easy to let slide. Skip the pre-manufacturing readiness check, and the first sign of trouble is usually a shutter that doesn't sit flush against a wall that's two centimetres different from what was originally measured, discovered on installation day, when fixing it costs time and sometimes a remake. Skip the mid-installation checkpoint, and a hinge alignment issue that would have taken five minutes to correct mid-build becomes something the installation team has to work around rather than fix properly, because by the time anyone notices, the surrounding panels are already in place.

None of this requires more visits in general. It requires the right three or four visits happening on schedule, which is a much easier thing to hold a studio accountable for than a vague promise of "regular communication."

Questions worth asking before you sign

  • "Who takes the measurement visit, you personally or a junior team member?" Either can work well, but you should know which it is, since the person measuring is the one whose accuracy the entire project depends on.
  • "What happens if the pre-manufacturing check finds something's changed on site?" A studio with a real process has a straightforward answer: measurements get updated and manufacturing proceeds from the corrected numbers. A studio without one usually hasn't thought that far ahead.
  • "If I can't be present for a visit, what do I get instead?" Photos, a short video, a written note, any of these work. No answer at all is the one to be wary of.

Frequently asked questions

Is it a red flag if my designer only visits twice during the whole project?

Not on its own. The measurement visit and the site-readiness check before manufacturing are the two that genuinely cannot be skipped. What matters more than the total count is whether those specific visits happened, and whether you received video or photo updates in between, rather than silence.

Can I just skip the pre-manufacturing site-readiness visit to save time?

You can, but it's the single highest-risk visit to skip. Manufacturing is based on measurements taken earlier, and if anything on site has changed since, electrical points moved, plastering finished slightly differently than planned, the carcass gets built to specifications that no longer match the actual wall.

Why does my designer need to be on-site for the full installation, not just at the start and end?

They don't, and shouldn't be. A good installation team manages the day-to-day fitting themselves. The designer's job is to check in at defined checkpoints, not supervise every hour, which is exactly why a project with someone present every single day is often a sign of a less experienced execution team, not more attentive service.

Does this visit schedule change for an NRI client managing the project remotely?

The visits themselves stay the same, what changes is how they're documented. For an NRI client managing things remotely, each of these checkpoints should come with a video walkthrough rather than just an internal note, so you have the same visibility as someone physically present would.

The bottom line

A modular project doesn't need constant supervision to go well, and it doesn't run fine on total silence either. It needs a small number of visits that actually matter, done at the right moments, with real documentation filling the gaps between them. If a studio can walk you through exactly when those visits happen before you've even signed anything, that's usually a better signal than any number they quote you.
